How it works

Fifteen minutes, start to finish.

Under local anaesthetic, through a single opening, with no stitches to take out afterwards. Here is the whole thing, minute by minute.

00:00

The local anaesthetic goes in

A local anaesthetic is injected into the area using a fine needle. It feels no worse than a flu injection or a dentist’s needle.

00:02

One small opening

A tiny hole is made in the scrotum. No scalpel is used, and no stitches will be needed to close it.

00:05

The vas is divided, and left open

The vas deferens is divided and the testicular end of the tube is left open. This helps prevent congestion in the testis, and so reduces the risk of pain or post-vasectomy syndrome.

00:08

A tissue barrier is placed

A tiny layer of tissue is placed between the two ends of the vas to stop them re-joining.

00:12

The other side, through the same opening

The same procedure is performed on the other side — reached through the single opening already made.

00:15

Closed and dressed

The skin edge is clipped together without stitches and a dressing is applied. Most men drive themselves home.

A vasectomy is a simple procedure where the vas deferens (the vas) is cut to cause sterilisation in a male. The vas is a tube that carries sperm from the testicles, where it is made, to the penis. On the way, sperm is joined by semen, so your ejaculate contains both.

Sperm makes up a very small percentage of that — less than 5%. Because we are only stopping the sperm, most men notice no change in the volume of their ejaculate after a vasectomy.

A vasectomy takes between 15 and 20 minutes, depending on the procedure.

There are two ways to categorise a vasectomy procedure.

Traditional or no-scalpel. The traditional method uses a scalpel to make an incision on each side of the scrotum to access the vas. The no-scalpel technique involves a single access hole, using blunt dissection to reach the vas from both sides. The no-scalpel method carries less chance of complications like bruising and bleeding, and offers a quicker recovery.

Open-ended or closed-ended. An open-ended vasectomy leaves the testicular end of the vas open, allowing sperm release into the scrotum. This reduces congestion and pressure — think of a kinked hose on a running tap. A closed-ended vasectomy clamps the testicular end with a clip or suture. We perform the open-ended technique.

Yes. It is safe to drive home after a vasectomy.

We only offer local anaesthetic for our vasectomy procedure. If you would prefer sedation or a general anaesthetic, a urologist can arrange that on referral.

Yes, a vasectomy can be reversed — however the process is costly and is not covered by Medicare. If you find yourself asking this question, you may want more time before deciding on permanent contraception.

All surgical procedures carry some risk, and we do everything we can to reduce the rate of complications. We believe patients should be aware of all of them, and they are set out in full in your consent form.

Common symptoms afterwards include:

  • Bruising in the days following your vasectomy, which should fade after a week or so.
  • Mild pain and swelling, which should settle within a few days.

Less common complications include:

  • Scrotal haematoma — a large bruise within the scrotum. The chance of this is significantly reduced by following recovery instructions about lifting in the days afterwards. If your job involves manual handling, we suggest time off or light duties.
  • Infection — we follow strict infection control protocols. Infections are uncommon, usually mild, and treated with oral antibiotics.
  • Post-vasectomy pain syndrome (PVPS) — a complication that can arise at any time after a vasectomy, with no agreed cause. Pain often reduces naturally, but in rare cases a specialist may need to perform additional surgery or a reversal, which is not always successful.

A vasectomy does not mean immediate sterilisation. You must consider yourself fertile until a semen analysis confirms the vasectomy was a success. That analysis is performed about three months afterwards, to make sure all residual sperm has cleared.

It depends on whether your job involves heavy lifting. If it doesn't, you can return immediately. If it does, you may need time off or light duties for the first week. We can write a medical certificate if you need one.

Generally you can resume sexual activity after a week — but you must consider yourself fertile until we notify you otherwise.

Technically, a “laser” vasectomy does not exist. Some vasectomists use a hyfrecator to cut the vas, which may be where the confusion comes from — but it is not a laser.

There's good news and bad news here.

The bad news: you won't be able to use private health insurance for a vasectomy with us. That's because we perform our procedures in medical centres rather than private hospitals, and private health insurance is only of benefit when your procedure is performed in a hospital or day surgery by a urologist.

The good news: it will almost certainly work out more affordable with us anyway.

  • Private health usually requires you to pay an excess — often $500, sometimes $1,000.
  • Your urologist will almost certainly have a gap on their fee, ranging from a few hundred dollars to over $1,000.
  • Nearly all surgeons will want you asleep, which means an anaesthetist fee and another gap.

Add those three together and you can end up $2,000 or more out of pocket — with insurance. With us you know exactly what you'll pay: $597. No “it depends on your health fund”, no “check with the anaesthetist”. Just one fee you can plan around, with one of Australia's most experienced vasectomists.
